# Project Overview — Food Decision Sprint

## The Problem

Urban professionals face daily decision fatigue around food. The problem has multiple intersecting layers:

- **Health restrictions** — dietary constraints due to medical conditions or fitness goals
- **Religious observance** — rules like eating vegetarian on specific days of the week
- **Cognitive overload** — not enough mental energy at the end of a workday to decide what to eat or instruct a cook
- **Joyless tracking** — existing apps force calorie counting and measurement, which turns eating into a chore rather than a pleasure
- **Shortcut behaviour** — when tired, people default to unhealthy or unsatisfying choices

The gap: no existing solution gives personalised, contextually aware food suggestions with near-zero daily input — and makes the experience enjoyable.

---

## Primary User

**The Urban Indian Corporate Professional**

| Attribute | Detail |
|---|---|
| Work pattern | 9-to-5 office job, commutes 3–5 days/week, metro city |
| Home setup | Has a cook, or cooks themselves on some days |
| Meal rhythm | Breakfast at home or office · Home-cooked or canteen lunch · Evening tea/snack · Dinner at home |
| Food sourcing | Home cooking + food delivery apps on busy nights · Eating out on weekends |
| Goals | Stay healthy, meet fitness or dietary goals, enjoy food |
| Pain | Too tired to decide; existing tools feel like work |

---

## Sprint Goal

Design and validate a prototype that:

1. **Surfaces what to eat** — suggestions tailored to the user's health, dietary rules, and preferences, applicable to home cooking, cook instructions, or food delivery
2. **Minimises daily input** — the system does the heavy lifting; users should not need to log, measure, or configure daily
3. **Enables light progress tracking** — users can see how they're doing against their goals without obsessing over numbers
4. **Feels fun and delightful** — people should want to open it, not feel obligated to

**North star:** People love using this and it feels nothing like a diet app.

---

## Constraints

| Constraint | Detail |
|---|---|
| Team | 2 product designers |
| Timeline | 2 weeks |
| Tech | No engineering support — prototype must be no-code or low-code |
| Output | A shareable, testable prototype suitable for PMF validation |
| Scope | Concept and interaction design only; no production build |

---

## Key Assumptions to Validate

These are the make-or-break beliefs that the sprint must stress-test:

1. **Demand assumption** — People actually want and need this solution (the problem is painful enough to seek a product)
2. **Behaviour change assumption** — People will choose this over consulting a nutritionist or dietician
3. **Monetisation assumption** — People will pay for this (the solution has commercial viability)

---

## Out of Scope

- No explicit "out of scope" constraints defined — sprint team should apply judgment based on the two-week, no-code constraint
- Avoid features that require daily manual input from the user (this contradicts the core proposition)

---

## Open Questions for the Sprint

- What is the minimum information we need to onboard a user (health goals, restrictions, preferences) without overwhelming them?
- How do we handle the cook workflow specifically — how does the suggestion reach the cook?
- What does "progress tracking" look like when you're not counting calories?
- What's the monetisation model to test — subscription, freemium, one-time?
- How do we differentiate the experience from MyFitnessPal, HealthifyMe, or similar apps users may have abandoned?

---

- [ ] User persona validated with at least 3–5 interviews
- [ ] Core user journey mapped (from morning decision to meal)
- [ ] Prototype built and ready to share
- [ ] PMF signal collected from at least 5 target users
