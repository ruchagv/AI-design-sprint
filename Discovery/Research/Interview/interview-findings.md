# Interview Findings — Food Decision Making
**Sprint:** Food Decision AI Sprint  
**Participants:** 5 (anonymised as P1–P5)  
**Data sources:** Interview transcripts + pre-interview questionnaires  
**Date compiled:** August 2026

---

## Participant Key (Anonymised)

| Code | Profile |
|------|---------|
| P1 | Lives alone, cook recently left, vegetarian / Jain dietary preference, orders delivery frequently |
| P2 | Lives with flatmates, has a cook, fitness-focused, no dietary restrictions |
| P3 | Lives alone, has a cook, fitness-focused, flexible/easygoing about food |
| P4 | Lives alone, has a cook, no dietary restrictions, repeats familiar meals weekly |
| P5 | Lives with one flatmate, has a cook, fitness goal (weight gain), prefers home food strongly |

---

## 1. Pain Points

### PP-01 — Decision fatigue when choosing what to order
**Description:** When no food is ready at home, users face overwhelming choice paralysis scrolling through delivery apps. The paradox of too many options — especially with repeated cuisine types — leads to frustration and delayed decisions.

**Supporting quote:**
> "Sometimes, you know, when you're hungry but you don't really know what you're hungry for. So it takes a lot of like scrolling time, then you're also getting hangry because you can't decide." — *P1*

**Interviews:** P1, P2, P4  
**Frequency:** 3 out of 5 interviews ★★★

---

### PP-02 — Mental load of deciding what to tell the cook
**Description:** Users with cooks face a recurring, low-grade cognitive burden every morning: deciding what to ask the cook to make before the cook arrives. When nothing comes to mind — especially early in the morning — it causes stress or defaults to the same safe dishes.

**Supporting quote:**
> "The stress for me mostly is related to thinking about what to tell her to cook for me. So basically deciding on what to have, what to ask her to cook, if there are like what things are present in my house right now." — *P4*

> "Very recently, it would be mostly in the mornings. Like when I wake up, I'm not fully woken up. So to decide at that point of time to put your mind into it, it is a bit of a struggle." — *P4*

**Interviews:** P1, P4, P5  
**Frequency:** 3 out of 5 interviews ★★★

---

### PP-03 — Tension between eating what you want and what is healthy
**Description:** Multiple participants described a constant internal conflict: choosing between food that tastes good and food that is nutritionally appropriate for their health or fitness goals. This tension is a source of ongoing dissatisfaction — they end up eating food that is neither fully satisfying nor fully aligned with health goals.

**Supporting quote:**
> "It is still is. There is always this balance of what I what I want to eat or what I would like eating versus what I should eat." — *P2*

> "I just end up eating healthy, not-so-tasty food or unhealthy food." — *P2 (survey)*

**Interviews:** P1, P2, P4  
**Frequency:** 3 out of 5 interviews ★★★

---

### PP-04 — Repetition and lack of food variety
**Description:** Participants relying on a cook or a limited grocery set tend to cycle through the same dishes week after week — not always because they enjoy them, but because it's convenient and they know the ingredients are available. This leads to food boredom over time.

**Supporting quote:**
> "There are certain things that you keep repeating every week. And the repetition of that food item is not because sometimes not because you like it very much. It is because that it's always convenient." — *P4*

> "There are very few places with a lot of vegetarian cuisine, it doesn't have like too much variety anyways, so options are limited. But in that also, there's a lot of repetition." — *P1*

**Interviews:** P1, P4, P5  
**Frequency:** 3 out of 5 interviews ★★★

---

### PP-05 — Inability to follow through on structured diet plans
**Description:** Several participants tried diet apps or consulted nutritionists but were unable to stick to the structured plans beyond a few weeks. Reasons include the rigidity of the plan clashing with shared cooking arrangements, low motivation over time, and the effort required to maintain the routine.

**Supporting quote:**
> "I wasn't able to follow through it after like 3 weeks." — *P5*

> "Since the cook makes for me and my flatmate... sometimes she would want, let's make this, then I'll also agree to it... I think that used to clash sometimes, and one was just a bit of laziness." — *P5*

**Interviews:** P1, P4, P5  
**Frequency:** 3 out of 5 interviews ★★★

---

### PP-06 — Manual food tracking is too effortful to sustain
**Description:** Apps that require daily logging — photos, calorie entries, manual inputs — feel like an added chore. Users tried them, but the discipline required was too high, and they dropped the habit quickly.

**Supporting quote:**
> "There's like a lot of discipline involved in tracking anything. And I think I was lacking that and also the time to do that. Sometimes I would just eat and then realise, oh, I've not really added anything or I forgot to take a picture." — *P1*

> "No, lot of manual entry." — *P1 (survey, reason for stopping Healthify Me)*

> "I have tried but I have never been consistent with it." — *P3*

**Interviews:** P1, P3, P4  
**Frequency:** 3 out of 5 interviews ★★★

---

### PP-07 — Coordinating meals with flatmates adds friction
**Description:** Participants who live with flatmates — and share a cook — face coordination overhead: deciding a shared meal that satisfies everyone, or navigating differing dietary goals within the same household. This makes personalised diet plans hard to execute.

**Supporting quote:**
> "There are 2 of them who actively manage the cooking... we are consulted or we just know that this is going to be cooked if there is anything specific that we want." — *P2*

> "To have her make something separate for me and something else for the rest of the people living, that was the first challenge." — *P4*

**Interviews:** P2, P4, P5  
**Frequency:** 3 out of 5 interviews ★★★

---

### PP-08 — Disappointment when ordered food is neither tasty nor healthy
**Description:** Ordering food from outside creates dual risk — the food may be unhealthy AND disappointing in taste. When both conditions are true simultaneously, users feel the worst about their food decision. The expectation of at least getting the taste right is often unmet.

**Supporting quote:**
> "Even more worse or the worst of the day is when I have ordered food from outside... and they are not neither tasteful and nor healthy. I feel terrible about after eating that because that just straight up sucks." — *P2*

> "I feel I'm very disappointed when the food is not good... it should be proportionate to what you're paying." — *P1*

**Interviews:** P1, P2  
**Frequency:** 2 out of 5 interviews ★★

## 2. Unmet Needs

### UN-01 — Personalised meal suggestions that fit real-world constraints
**Description:** Users want food recommendations that account for what is actually available in their fridge or pantry, their dietary preferences, health goals, and — critically — the ingredients a shared cook can realistically work with. Generic diet plans or restaurant menus don't meet this need.

**Supporting quote:**
> "This app I liked because it asked me what are the things that I usually have and usually order as a grocery for myself and devised a diet plan based on that." — *P4*

> "If it can curate and like suggest what I can probably eat and possible recipes from social media... that would be great." — *P2*

**Interviews:** P2, P4, P5  
**Frequency:** 3 out of 5 interviews ★★★

---

### UN-02 — A nutrition plan that is flexible, not rigid
**Description:** Participants want nutritional guidance or a meal plan but need it to flex with mood, availability, and social context — not a fixed day-by-day schedule that breaks the moment something changes.

**Supporting quote:**
> "If I get that, I that would definitely be a relief for me as long as that is flexible enough. Like I don't want like there might be a few recommendations for a week... but I think it also sometimes it also depends on my mood." — *P3*

> "I would still to have some freedom to tweak the diet plan and everything." — *P3*

**Interviews:** P2, P3, P5  
**Frequency:** 3 out of 5 interviews ★★★

---

### UN-03 — Clarity on what nutrients are in everyday Indian food
**Description:** Users are interested in knowing the nutritional content of the meals they already eat — especially common Indian dishes like dal, roti, chai, chapati — but don't have easy access to that information in a usable format.

**Supporting quote:**
> "So if I wanted to find how much calories like a cup of chai has, so I just used to... there were a lot of these customised dietary things there, so I could see some options like one cup of chai it has around 125 or 150 calories." — *P3*

> "If someone can help me to make a plan, this chart for me, which has the exact amount of these nutrients... that would be really helpful for me." — *P3*

**Interviews:** P1, P3  
**Frequency:** 2 out of 5 interviews ★★

---

### UN-04 — Motivation and accountability to stay on track
**Description:** Users acknowledge they know what they should eat but struggle with follow-through. They want an external nudge — accountability, check-ins, or even gamification — to keep them engaged beyond the initial motivation spike.

**Supporting quote:**
> "I think since I live with two other girls and I have a cook coming in... maybe me or if the app could keep me more motivated, then I would have kept on using it maybe. I don't know, make a game out of it." — *P4*

> "One drawback of that app was it wouldn't ask me to come back and say whether I did it or not, or check up on." — *P4*

**Interviews:** P1, P4  
**Frequency:** 2 out of 5 interviews ★★

---

### UN-05 — Help deciding what groceries to order
**Description:** Multiple participants — especially those with cooks — said that the decision of what vegetables and groceries to order was a recurring friction point. They default to the same items not because they prefer them, but because it's the path of least resistance.

**Supporting quote:**
> "Ordering vegetables is something I find like [difficult]... I don't know what sabzi to order and I end up maybe just buying like those two, three sabzis that I usually eat." — *P5*

**Interviews:** P1, P5  
**Frequency:** 2 out of 5 interviews ★★

---

## 3. Workarounds

### WA-01 — Delegating all cooking decisions to flatmates or the cook
**Description:** When users don't want to think about food, they defer entirely to whoever is managing the kitchen — a more engaged flatmate, a cook with standing instructions, or family members. This removes cognitive burden but also removes personal control.

**Supporting quote:**
> "The standing order for the cook was that just make whatever is there in the fridge, like we wouldn't even give the instructions." — *P2*

> "There are 2 of them who actively manage the cooking and what is going to be cooked... they mostly take care of what is going to be cooked." — *P2*

**Interviews:** P2, P5  
**Frequency:** 2 out of 5 interviews ★★

---

### WA-02 — Building a personal system of rotating vegetables/meals
**Description:** Rather than planning explicitly, some users create informal systems — buying a rotation of 4–5 vegetables for the week, giving the cook simple rules (don't repeat the same dish two days in a row), or establishing a rough weekly cadence.

**Supporting quote:**
> "Five different kind of vegetables for five different days, something like that, so then she can just pick and do one rotation... the instructions were simple: don't repeat every day the same value and don't repeat every day the same vegetable." — *P1*

**Interviews:** P1, P4  
**Frequency:** 2 out of 5 interviews ★★

---

### WA-03 — Asking AI (ChatGPT / Claude) for on-demand food advice
**Description:** Multiple participants have started using AI tools ad hoc for food-related questions — checking whether a food is healthy, getting recipe ideas, or building a rough diet plan — as a low-friction alternative to tracking apps or nutritionists.

**Supporting quote:**
> "AI is like very on demand. I don't have to do it every single day, right? It's not a compulsion." — *P1*

> "I was using Claude... I was trying to make like a diet chart for me for like a weekly diet chart." — *P3*

> "Definitely, when I feel very guilty about eating anything, I will check with [AI]." — *P1*

**Interviews:** P1, P3  
**Frequency:** 2 out of 5 interviews ★★

---

### WA-04 — Using a food tracking app briefly around a specific event
**Description:** Users are most likely to adopt a diet app or meal plan when there is a clear, time-bound goal (e.g., a wedding to attend, a fitness target). Without that external trigger, motivation fades quickly.

**Supporting quote:**
> "These kinds of things for me at least is very occasion specific. I remember I started using it because there was a wedding that I had to attend, or there was something like that." — *P4*

**Interviews:** P4  
**Frequency:** 1 out of 5 interviews ★

---

### WA-05 — Monitoring health via annual checkups and acting on results
**Description:** Rather than continuous dietary tracking, some users do periodic health checkups and make targeted dietary adjustments based on the results (e.g., cutting oil when cholesterol was high, reducing sugar when diabetes risk appeared).

**Supporting quote:**
> "I also do yearly checkups to see like where am I going. So once my cholesterol was towards the higher side, so that's I started like cutting off on oils... once it was more leaning towards diabetes, so I just cut off sugar." — *P1*

**Interviews:** P1  
**Frequency:** 1 out of 5 interviews ★

---

### WA-06 — Searching online / social media for food ideas
**Description:** Users discover recipe or food inspiration informally through YouTube, Instagram, or Google searches — then pass that idea to their cook or plan to cook it themselves. This is ad hoc and not integrated with any planning or nutrition framework.

**Supporting quote:**
> "If I just feel like eating something, I would go find a recipe and send that over to the cook." — *P2*

> "I do get my food ideas from family, sometimes from social media also, and most of the times from YouTube." — *P2*

**Interviews:** P2, P4  
**Frequency:** 2 out of 5 interviews ★★

## 4. Desired Outcomes

### DO-01 — Eat healthy most days without having to think hard about it
**Description:** The primary aspiration across participants is to eat well on most weekdays with minimal daily mental effort. A successful week means healthy food 5 out of 7 days — without the cognitive overhead of planning, deciding, and negotiating every meal.

**Supporting quote:**
> "A successful week would be me eating healthy every, like at least five days when I'm working. Maybe on Saturday, Sunday I can take some, you know, one or two meals from outside." — *P1*

> "I like this idea of not having to think about like what to eat every day, especially on those days when I am busy." — *P3*

**Interviews:** P1, P2, P3, P4  
**Frequency:** 4 out of 5 interviews ★★★★

---

### DO-02 — Meals that are both tasty AND healthy (not a compromise)
**Description:** Users don't want to choose between enjoyment and nutrition. The ideal outcome is regularly cracking meals that satisfy both criteria simultaneously — and building a reliable repertoire of such meals rather than defaulting to either junk food or bland healthy food.

**Supporting quote:**
> "It would be things when we really crack something that is like really tasty to eat but is outstandingly healthy as well. So being able to crack like we did crack some chicken fajitas the other day... it is healthy, it is tasty, and if it is easy to prepare, nothing like it." — *P2*

**Interviews:** P1, P2, P3  
**Frequency:** 3 out of 5 interviews ★★★

---

### DO-03 — Someone (or something) to take over the planning entirely
**Description:** Multiple participants said, when asked what one thing they'd want offloaded, they pointed to the planning and deciding step — knowing what to eat, correlating it with what is available, and building out a ready-to-execute list. They don't want to think about it at all.

**Supporting quote:**
> "Not sure if I have to take, it would be the part where I have to know what I have to eat, what I should eat. That's the one. And correlating it to what possible things I can eat... If that part could be tackled, nothing like it." — *P2*

> "It would be mostly the planning on what to eat... the deciding part of like what to eat." — *P3*

> "I really wish there could be just like an easier way to have food... I get really tired of just eating." — *P5*

**Interviews:** P2, P3, P4, P5  
**Frequency:** 4 out of 5 interviews ★★★★

---

### DO-04 — Feel guilt-free about the food they eat
**Description:** Users want to feel confident and at peace with their food choices — not second-guessing whether they've eaten the right things. The best food experiences are described as "guilt-free" — when nutrition and enjoyment align naturally.

**Supporting quote:**
> "She was making food for me and it felt good because I was enjoying the food at the same time I knew and it was guilt free because I knew that it was home cooked." — *P4*

> "I would really like to shift to a completely healthy diet. But you know, there are like, I don't really enjoy that too much." — *P1*

**Interviews:** P1, P4  
**Frequency:** 2 out of 5 interviews ★★

---

### DO-05 — A structured but achievable nutrient plan personalised to fitness goals
**Description:** Fitness-conscious participants want a concrete, calculated plan that maps their macro goals (protein, carbs, fat, fibre) to actual food they can eat — without needing to become a nutritionist themselves or follow an impractical meal plan.

**Supporting quote:**
> "If someone can help me to make a plan, this chart for me, which has the exact amount of these nutrients... let us say the fiber is also important, so my food has some fiber into it, good fats, what else, these carbs, proteins. If I can get a properly calculated this nutrients chart, I would also like it, it will be really helpful for me." — *P3*

**Interviews:** P2, P3  
**Frequency:** 2 out of 5 interviews ★★
